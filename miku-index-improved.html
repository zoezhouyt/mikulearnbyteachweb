02 / The learning loop

Thinking becomes something you can inspect.

Instead of correcting the learner immediately, Miku makes their explanation do the work.

Each step externalises a different part of metacognition: explaining a mental model, estimating what that model can support, seeing where it fails, and deliberately revising it.

01→

Explain

Put what you think you know into your own words. Miku only receives what you actually teach.

02→

Model

Your explanation becomes a visible network of concepts and relationships rather than a hidden chat history.

03→

Predict

Before the quiz, decide where Miku will succeed and fail. Commit to a judgment before seeing the outcome.

04

Revise

Use the mismatch between prediction and performance to decide what needs to be clarified, connected or re-taught.

03 / Make the mental model visible

Not a score. A structure.

A learner may feel that they “know seasons” while missing the relationship between tilt, sunlight and orbit. Miku turns those relationships into something visible enough to question.

The goal is not to label a learner as right or wrong. It is to show where an explanation is connected, where it is thin, and how it changes after new evidence.

04 / Learning science

The design is built around a few specific bets.

Miku does not try to wrap every learning theory into one product. The prototype focuses on four ideas that directly change the interaction.

01

Explaining exposes gaps.

The learner must generate an explanation rather than recognise one. Missing links become harder to hide when the idea has to be produced in their own words.

Chi et al., 1989; 1994
02

A pupil changes the motivation.

Miku is positioned as someone to help, not an authority evaluating the learner. Its mistakes become problems to repair rather than marks against the learner.

Chase, Chin, Oppezzo & Schwartz, 2009
03

Prediction makes confidence testable.

Learners commit to what they think Miku knows before the quiz. The prediction can then be compared with what actually happens.

Nelson & Narens, 1990
04

Failure creates a reason to revise.

Instead of supplying a correction immediately, Miku lets a learner encounter the consequences of an incomplete explanation and then reconstruct it.

Posner, Strike, Hewson & Gertzog, 1982

Core constraint: Miku should behave like a faithfully ignorant pupil. If the learner never taught it something, Miku should not quietly answer from the base model's own knowledge.

Inspired by Betty's Brain

Learning by teaching, reimagined for conversational AI.

Miku takes inspiration from Betty's Brain, a teachable-agent environment developed at Vanderbilt University. In Betty's Brain, students teach an agent by building a causal map, then use its quiz performance to inspect the quality of that map.

Miku keeps the same bargain — the pupil only knows what you taught it — but uses conversation and a generated knowledge structure so the learner has to articulate the explanation in their own words.

05 / Prototype questions

What would make this worth pursuing?

The prototype is useful only if the interaction produces observable changes in how learners monitor and revise their understanding. These are the first things I would test in a small pilot.

H1
Teaching a bounded pupil surfaces gaps that ordinary question-answering misses.
Look forConcepts or relations added only after Miku fails.
H2
Item-level prediction improves calibration within a learning session.
Look forAgreement between predicted and actual quiz outcomes.
H3
Re-teaching changes the structure of an explanation, not just its length.
Look forNew or corrected relationships in the knowledge map.
H4
The most important technical condition is fidelity to the teaching log.
Look forMiku admitting ignorance on untaught-question probes.
06 / Why now

When answers are cheap, judgment becomes scarce.

Generative AI removes much of the friction that used to force learners to explain, struggle and repair an idea. Miku is an experiment in designing that cognitive work back into the interaction — deliberately.
